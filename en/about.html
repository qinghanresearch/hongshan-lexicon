About the lexicon

Reading the lexicon

Begin with a word, identify its referent, explore its naming history, and compare translation choices. Follow linked terms through the text or switch to the same entry in Chinese using the language control.

Reading and translating in both directions

The English interface serves international readers of Hongshan research and translators working from English or other languages into Chinese. Chinese characters, relevant pinyin, original terms, and Chinese publication titles remain part of the explanation. They allow readers to examine a Chinese expression directly, rather than relying on an English gloss alone.

Translation works in both directions here, but the choices need not be symmetrical. For example, barrow and tumulus may refer to 墓冢; neither automatically identifies the specifically stone-built monument described by 积石冢. The object, context, naming tradition, and the author’s argument matter when choosing a Chinese term.

This public reading edition

The edition contains 20 bilingual entries organized within the project's eleven categories. Categories without entries show zero; the full dictionary corpus is not yet online. New material includes English burial-mound terminology, microlithic classification, and Hongshan naming in texts associated with Su Bingqi.

Chinese exposition and English prose were prepared with AI assistance and await scholarly editorial review. The status of an existing translation decision is distinct from the review status of its new explanation or English presentation. This is not a final edition with every primary citation verified.

Evidence and interpretation

Each entry names its references and the scope actually checked. A citation recorded in an editorial dossier does not imply that every original excavation report has been re-examined. Abstract-only checks are identified. Disputed interpretations retain their attribution and qualifications.

Updates

Stable entry identifiers pair the two languages. Publication regenerates pages, search, and cross-references from editorial content. Readers do not need to see Markdown, and scholarly prose is not generated on demand while they read.

About the editor

Qing Han 韩晴 is a researcher trained in social anthropology at the University of Oxford, with interests in knowledge, technology, and social life. Han’s work on cultural heritage translation examines Hongshan heritage, the history of archaeological categories, and translation practices.
